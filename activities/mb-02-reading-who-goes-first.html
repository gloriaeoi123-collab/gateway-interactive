Who Goes First?

Gateway · MB (B2–C1) · 751 words · Feature article

At most busy junctions, the instructions are unmistakable. Red means stop. Green means go. White lines tell pedestrians where to cross, while road markings keep vehicles in their designated lanes. The entire system is designed to reduce uncertainty. But what would happen if many of those instructions suddenly disappeared?

In the Dutch town of Drachten, traffic engineer Hans Monderman helped put that question to the test. In the early 2000s, a busy junction known as Laweiplein underwent a remarkable transformation. The traffic lights were removed, many conventional road markings disappeared, and the area was redesigned as something between a public square and a roundabout.

There were fountains, paving stones and a small central area of grass. Cars, cyclists and pedestrians still needed to pass through, but the familiar signals telling them precisely when to move were largely gone. Instead of following a sequence of instructions, road users had to pay much closer attention to one another.

At first sight, the idea seemed almost irresponsible. Traffic lights exist for a reason. Why remove something intended to prevent accidents, particularly at a junction carrying thousands of vehicles every day?

Monderman's reasoning turned conventional thinking on its head. He believed that excessive regulation could encourage drivers to stop making decisions for themselves. A green light, for example, might give motorists a sense of security even when other road users were behaving unpredictably. Remove that reassurance, he argued, and drivers might become more cautious rather than less.

His approach became associated with a concept known as shared space. Instead of treating streets primarily as channels through which vehicles must move efficiently, shared-space design attempts to make them places where different users interact. The physical environment itself—its appearance, layout and materials—encourages drivers to slow down and negotiate their movements.

The early results in Drachten attracted considerable attention. Traffic continued to flow, often without the long queues previously associated with the lights. Accident figures also fell following the redesign. To supporters, it appeared to demonstrate something counterintuitive: making a road feel less predictable might actually encourage safer behaviour.

Yet interpreting the results was not quite so simple. The junction had undergone more than the removal of traffic lights. Its entire layout had changed, including the introduction of a roundabout-like arrangement. How much of the improvement could therefore be attributed to increased attention, and how much to the new road design? The figures alone could not settle that question.

There was another difficulty, and it concerned the very people the scheme was supposed to benefit.

Shared space depends heavily on observation, judgement and informal communication. Drivers may slow down after seeing a pedestrian hesitate; cyclists may negotiate their way through traffic with a glance or a gesture. But what happens when somebody cannot see those signals?

Organisations representing blind and partially sighted people have raised serious objections to shared-space schemes, particularly where conventional kerbs and controlled crossings have been removed. For someone who relies on physical boundaries to distinguish the pavement from the road, an open, apparently continuous surface can be difficult to navigate. Eye contact is of little use to a pedestrian who cannot see the driver approaching.

Nor are these concerns limited to visual impairment. Older pedestrians, young children and people who find unpredictable traffic situations difficult may prefer clearly defined spaces and unambiguous instructions. A layout that encourages one person to become more attentive may leave another feeling exposed.

This creates an uncomfortable distinction between two ideas that are often treated as equivalent: a road that records fewer accidents and a road that everybody can use confidently. The first can be assessed through traffic data. The second also requires an understanding of the experiences of those who use the space—or decide to avoid it.

None of this necessarily means that Monderman's experiment was misguided. His work challenged an important assumption: that safety always improves when more instructions, signs and barriers are introduced. Streets communicate through their design, and sometimes a change in the environment can influence behaviour more effectively than another warning sign.

But the experience also suggests that removing instructions does not affect everyone equally. Some people respond to uncertainty by becoming more careful. Others may find that the same uncertainty limits their freedom to move around independently.

Perhaps the real question raised by Drachten is not whether streets should have fewer rules. It is whether a system can encourage people to take greater responsibility for their actions without making others depend on their goodwill—or their ability to see it.
