For more than a century, a sizeable island appeared on maps of the Pacific. In 2012, researchers went looking for it. What they found was a problem with the maps themselves.

The Island That Wasn’t There

Gateway · B2 · 722 words · Narrative non-fiction

In October 2012, a group of scientists travelling across the Pacific Ocean came across something rather unusual. According to their maps, an island lay directly ahead of them. It was approximately 25 kilometres long and five kilometres wide — hardly the sort of place anyone could overlook. Yet the navigation charts on board their ship suggested that the water in the same area was around 1,400 metres deep. Both sources could not possibly be right.

The scientists were aboard the Southern Surveyor, a research vessel collecting information about the ocean floor. Their original mission had nothing to do with investigating mysterious islands. However, the disagreement between their maps was too intriguing to ignore, so they decided to take a closer look.

As the ship approached the location, there was no sign of land. No coastline appeared on the horizon, and the instruments continued to indicate deep water. The crew had reached the precise position where an island was supposed to stand, but there was nothing there except open sea.

What made the discovery particularly surprising was that this was not an island mentioned only in an ancient document. Known as Sandy Island, it appeared on modern digital maps, including Google Earth, and in geographical databases used by scientists. How could such a considerable piece of land remain on maps when it apparently did not exist?

To understand the mystery, researchers had to travel backwards in time.

The earliest known record associated with Sandy Island dates from 1876, when a whaling ship called the Velocity sailed through the region. Its crew reportedly observed what appeared to be small sandy islands or dangerous areas of breaking waves. At a time when navigation depended heavily on observations made at sea, such reports were valuable. A captain who noticed a possible hazard had good reason to record it, even if its exact position could not be established.

The problem was that identifying an object in the distance was not always straightforward. Poor visibility, changing weather conditions and inaccurate calculations could easily lead to mistakes. Something that appeared to be land might turn out to be a reef, an unusual formation of waves or simply an object seen from the wrong position.

Whatever the original sailors encountered, the supposed island eventually found its way onto official charts. A British Admiralty chart published in 1908 included Sandy Island, linking it to the nineteenth-century report. From that point onwards, the geographical mystery became a problem of information rather than exploration.

Mapmakers frequently relied on earlier charts when preparing new editions. This was perfectly reasonable: nobody could personally examine every stretch of coastline before publishing a map of the world. Consequently, information was passed from one generation of maps to the next. Unfortunately, errors could be passed on in exactly the same way.

Not everyone accepted Sandy Island's existence without question. Some charts marked it as doubtful, while others left it out altogether. Nevertheless, the island survived in certain collections of geographical information. When these collections were transferred into digital databases, the old mistake acquired a surprisingly modern appearance. A feature originally recorded with considerable uncertainty could now be displayed as a clearly defined shape on a computer screen.

This is perhaps the most interesting aspect of the story. Digital maps may look extraordinarily precise, but their appearance does not necessarily reveal the quality of the information behind them. Much of that information has been gathered over centuries, sometimes under conditions very different from those in which it is used today.

The scientists' discovery attracted international attention, and Sandy Island was subsequently removed from major digital maps. Researchers also examined its history, attempting to establish how the original report had survived for so long. Although its precise origins remain uncertain, the explanation seems to involve a combination of an early navigational mistake and the repeated copying of geographical data.

In one sense, the expedition achieved something rather unusual. Most explorers become famous for discovering places that nobody previously knew existed. The scientists aboard the Southern Surveyor had done almost the opposite: they had demonstrated that a place familiar to generations of mapmakers was not there at all.

And perhaps the strangest detail is that Sandy Island had never needed to exist in order to travel around the world. For more than a century, it had been doing so on paper.
